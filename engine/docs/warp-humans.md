# Warp Humans stadium review

Play a full desktop Growing Guns match with one local player, three bots and the procedural spectators:

```sh
cargo run --release -p growing-guns-app --bin growing-guns -- play --skip-menu --seed 52
```

In this mode each player slot and bot gets a Warp Human from the arena seed. The game fixes standing height at 1.80 m and assigns the slot's shirt colour. Faces, hair, skin, shoulder breadth, waist and hips vary. The wardrobe selects a pullover, a short-sleeved T-shirt or a jacket, then colours trousers and shoes independently. A chest can be plain, have a two-digit number or carry a wordmark. The match binds the fitted body and clothing to the player skeleton at load time, so ordinary movement, hand sockets and ragdolls use the same model across respawns.

When Splitscreen Club prepares a match, the seated players wear the sRGB shirt colour and skin tone from their profiles. The face texture keeps its features and uses the same base skin tone as the arms and hands. Each missing colour uses its own fallback. The effective skin colour weights the seeded natural hair palette for players and spectators. Darker skin makes black and dark brown more frequent, while the full range through honey and pale blond remains possible. A separate seed draw gives 6 in 256 people a muted dye and keeps their brows in the natural palette. These are art-direction weights. Colour edits from the lobby refresh the visuals for the active match. The roster seed stays fixed across graphics changes.

The third-person reload uses the weapon's reload progress. The primary hand lifts the rifle while the support hand reaches its current magazine, withdraws it, reseats it and returns to its usual grip. The same contacts work for a growing magazine or drum. The leg pose continues underneath the action.

Before a Growing Guns round launches, each player samples the spectator nearest their starting place. `Instance::standing_pose` takes that seat, the stadium clock and the live crowd mood. It returns the hop in metres and the body and wrist pose for a unit-height Human. The player scales that pose to their rig. The first-person hands and the body seen by other players follow the same gesture until takeoff.

Warp Human players use Quaternius' walk, jog, sprint, jump start and landing clips from the engine's [body animation library](../crates/body/src/lib.rs). At roster load the game measures the supporting toe's backward travel on the actual player skeleton and aligns the three ground cycles at midstance. Each tick advances the blended cycle by the horizontal metres the capsule moved after collision. The legs follow the direction of travel and reverse their cycle when backing up. The close player keeps the relaxed hm08 shoulder, elbow and wrist positions. Both hands meet the current weapon contacts using their anatomical segment lengths. The gaze follows vertical aim through the neck and head. At rest the ankles settle about 0.30 m apart and the knees absorb the change while the soles stay planted. Render frames interpolate between tick phases.

Rising velocity drives the change from toe-off to tucked knees. As the player descends the feet extend toward contact. Ground contact starts the landing bend. A moving player blends back into the supporting stride immediately, while a stationary landing absorbs the impact with both legs.

Add `--start-in-stands` to begin the match on a tread facing a neighbour at eye level. A standard player is 1.80 m tall with the camera at 1.60 m. Seeded spectators range from 1.55 to 1.95 m. The game grounds them on the same stepped geometry used for walking.

Warp Humans matches default to packed seating with eight radial aisles. The generator spreads up to 15,000 seats over the whole bowl. Use `--crowd-density standard` for the earlier spacing. The stadium viewer and headless review accept both explicit settings and use the same packed default.

The match supplies the seat identities, live positions, crowd reactions and per-player camera. At rest the outer shoulders settle slightly and the fitted forearms unfold until the hands hang alongside the thighs. The stance narrows from hip to ankle while the soles stay level. Each arm releases that adjustment as it rises to cheer or react to a blast. A seeded jump cycle bends both knees before takeoff, swings the arms, tucks the feet in flight and lowers the hips after contact. Flight time follows the jump height. The crowd pose and hit tests use the same arc, and lateral escapes fade the jump into the stepping pose.

Shots use the simulated bullet path up to its first gameplay obstruction. The GPU tests a body-sized cylinder at the spectator's animated position. One bullet id can remove one living spectator across its segments. Nearby survivors have individual escape and return trajectories. Repeat gunfire extends an active escape or starts from the person's current displacement during the return. An explosion removes everyone in its lethal radius and scatters the surrounding survivors. Their hips, shoulders and head turn briefly away from the blast position, with a smaller turn near the edge of the scare radius. They then face the arena again. Another explosion redirects the turn from the current angle. Death persists across cameras and detail levels until the crowd is reset.

Confirmed contacts return asynchronously for directional blood spray, GPU debris and mist. A shared atlas supplies 16 blood footprints. A rough surface finish keeps the red visible when a stadium lamp reflects toward the camera. The fan at the hit and the falling droplets sample actual steps and walls. Selected rigid gibs stamp their first static contact. All decals reuse the stadium and arena mark pools. The game's ordinary frame loop never waits for these notifications. The CPU's reported number alive can lag the visible deaths. Run the in-stands scenario with `script games/growing-guns/scripts/crowd-reactions.json --out build/crowd-reactions --start-in-stands --warp-humans --seed 52 --graphics full --sky noon --players 1`.
A single spectator hit emits twelve tumbling pieces from the GPU debris pool. Their combined mesh volume scales with the victim's body dimensions, using 75 litres for a reference adult. Large blasts split a budget of 192 pieces among their victims. Three irregular shapes share a dark, fibrous cut-surface texture. Half the pieces have one outer skin face selected from eight tones using the spectator's pigmentation.

The people render into the arena's HDR colour and depth buffers before transparency and post-processing.
Each visible spectator receives the scene's sun shadow, floodlights and local lights at torso height during GPU detail selection. All detail levels share that sample; surface normals shape the response on faces, hair and clothing. Each visible surface samples the stadium's distance fog using its animated world position and the current camera. Shirts, faces and distant impostors take on the same atmospheric colour as adjacent masonry, including shadowed mist. The crowd does not cast individual shadows.
Desktop matches use Warp Humans for spectators, players and bots by default. Splitscreen Club profile colours apply to the player roster. Use `--no-warp-humans` for the older spectator renderer. The minimum graphics profile omits the stadium.

Launch the interactive desktop stadium viewer with:

```sh
cargo run --release -p growing-guns-app --bin growing-guns -- human-play --seed 52
```

Scroll to zoom, drag with the left mouse button to orbit, and use Left/Right
to select a neighbour. Home frames the stadium, End frames the face, Space
pauses the motion and Esc closes the playtest.

Run the repeatable headless review from the workspace root on a GPU with compute and indirect drawing.

```sh
cargo run --release -p growing-guns-app --bin growing-guns -- human-review --seed 52 --far cells --size 960x540 --frames 121 --out build/humans/cells
```

Repeat with `--far mesh` and `--far impostor` and separate output directories.
Each output frame contains the travelling camera on the left and the stationary
stadium camera on the right. Both sample the same presentation time and immutable
person table. Four frames and a JSON report are saved. Add `--sequence` to save
every frame for video. Use separate runs for video and timings.

Growing Guns supplies its bowl and occupied seats. A person's seed selects
proportions, pigmentation, clothing and movement phase. Engine callers can fix
height and shirt colour with `render::humans::Constraints`. Seats use the game's
height. The close character uses a fitted top and trousers, shoes, alpha-cutout hair and brows on the MakeHuman hm08 body. Four adult heads share the template and are registered at the midpoint of the eyes. A seed blends their complete proportions with nonnegative weights summing to one. Shoulder breadth fades out over the neck. The original skull and facial-joint weights keep the cheeks, nose and jaw together when the head turns. Short, bobbed and tightly curled grooms supply the base silhouette. A close camera also draws individual tapered hair strands. The GPU grows each curve from the seed and a shared 64 by 16 fitted guide field, transports it with the head and varies the free tips over presentation time. Player heads fade in up to 6,144 strands between projected head sizes of 24 and 142 pixels. The bob adds up to 10,240 finer strands between 130 and 280 pixels. Its part, wavy undercoat and face-framing locks follow the same scalp-fitted guide volume. Spectators above 70 head pixels use 1,536 strands. The indirect draws use vertex IDs and allocate no vertex or index buffer per strand. Hip, knee, shoulder and elbow motion uses the body's transferred skin weights. Each visible spectator gets a seeded sequence of quiet shifts, glances and cheering bouts. Either arm can lead. The collarbone follows a raised arm, the spine leans and twists around the waist, and the head turns and nods about the neck. When someone moves along a row, each foot alternates between a fixed landing point and a swinging phase driven by signed distance from the seat. A two-segment leg solve bends the hip and knee and rolls the hip toward the target. The ankle counter-rotates to keep the shoe level. Fear lowers the pelvis while limb lengths stay fixed. A nearby escape or a rise in stadium panic makes spectators throw up one or both hands. Short reaches vary in strength and timing per arm and per spectator. Their phase follows the presentation clock through repeated scares and detail changes. The same upper-body angles and lower-body pose drive all detail levels. Live match excitement and fear come from the crowd's existing pose uniform. Attribution and the
binary layout are in `engine/assets/humans/README.md`.

All spectators use one set of garment geometry. A vertex mask selects the outfit and the exposed skin. Shoe, trouser and shirt dyes fit in the existing 64-byte identity. At long range the GPU uses the large colour blocks. On the close model it combines fabric detail with glyphs from the shared atlas. Close players have smooth lighting across the deformed face, a shaped trouser cuff, a darker sole and a separate exposure for each textured groom. The player renderer uses the same outfit selection and glyphs, fits the chosen geometry at load time and shares the donor animation data across the roster.

In the stands, feet have a seeded fore-aft stagger. A lateral weight shift at the pelvis feeds into the two leg solves so the ankles stay on the tread. A side step also drives opposing shoulder movement and torso rotation.

The compute pass culls and classifies by projected height separately per
view. Below 32 pixels the test draws articulated boxes, low-sided meshes, or
camera-facing elliptical impostors. Up to 170 pixels it uses shared rounded
parts. The medium band starts background decoding of the anatomical mesh and appearance atlas. Beyond 170 pixels the GPU receives the shared detail page; medium
geometry covers any frames spent waiting for that page. The upload happens after both
views have rendered. Both cameras can then use the same resident mesh.
The embedded CPU reference stays available for the lifetime of the executable.
This experiment has one detail page and no eviction.

The report includes the selected instance counts, per-view GPU selection and
raster time when timestamp queries are supported, allocated geometry and work
buffer bytes, and request-to-first-detailed-frame latency. Atlas bytes and colour and depth
texture bytes are listed separately. Driver allocations are outside these totals.
Readback serializes views for reproducibility. PNG encoding and readback can
increase cold wall time. The fixture uses simple directional lighting and the
stadium geometry; gameplay, shadowing and the normal frame compositor are
outside its timing scope.

Compare the brow ridge, nose projection and distance from the lips to the chin
at equal camera angle. The close crowd shader shifts the iris, shades the upper part of the eye and
moves the actual lid vertices with a shared sparse closure target. Players use
two eye pivots and six lid translation axes in their GPU skin palettes. The
same seeded timing drives both rigs. The existing skin atlas carries a sheen
mask for forehead, nose and lips. Sun and stadium lamps determine eye highlights.
Fitted people (players and Luau humans) rest their lids a third of a closure wider than the cooked neutral, which reads half-asleep up close; the crowd keeps the cooked opening.
The rest of the face holds a neutral expression.

A fitted person (a player or a Luau human) gets their own face texture, grown from their seed when they are fitted (`assets::skinned::warp_skin`, about a second on four cores at 2048 texels). The painted face tile they share supplies the fall of colour over the head, the lips and the lids. Over it the seed adds the skin's relief as a normal map (millimetre polygons bounded by fine furrows, pores where they meet, lip creases, forehead lines and crow's feet with age) and its colour (shadow in pores, uneven redness on cheeks, nose and ears, freckles, moles). The relief is a function of the point on the head, so it crosses UV seams and keeps its size where the unwrap stretches. The same texture holds the person's eyes in a free corner: an iris with fibres, a collarette and a dark rim in a colour drawn from the seed (lighter colours only with fair skin), and a white that warms towards the corners. The crowd keeps the shared tiles.

Sunlight passing through thin skin reads the sun shadow map for the thickness in its way and blends the four texels round the point; one nearest texel printed the map's grid on a cheek turned from the sun.

A fitted person also grows their own hair from the seed (`assets::skinned::warp_hair`). Up to 16,384 strands of ten points root on the scalp of their own head, inside a hairline that thins out toward the forehead, drops at the temples to a sideburn, runs close over the ear and down behind it to the nape. The seed picks a cut (`warp_hair::Style`): buzz, tousled crop, side part, swept back, fringe, afro, curtains, bob, shoulder length and long. Short cuts are by far the most common; one person in nine has hair to the ears or longer and one in fifty has long hair. Most people with dark skin have tightly coiled hair: it grows in tufts a few millimetres across whose hairs wind together as one spiral, stands straight off the round of the head, kinks its own way as it grows and does not hang, and is worn as a buzz, a short cut with tapered sides or an afro. Ten points hold two or three turns of such a spiral, so of coiled hair longer than 2 cm each strand is drawn over one 2 cm stretch at its own depth: together they fill an afro with coils from the scalp to its surface. Within 2 cm of the hairline every strand is drawn from the skin instead, so the edge of the hair is coils growing out of the forehead and not a bare strip under the mass. Grown long the coils are up to 1.6 times larger and looser and the stretch as much longer, and coiled strands are drawn 1.8 times as wide (`Cut::width`, the groom's `weights.y`): the same strands have a far larger volume to fill, and at the width of straight hair the head showed through. Where coiled hair meets an ear or the neck it ends. It is darker than straight hair of the same seed. Short cuts are tapered: a few millimetres at the hairline over the ears and at the nape, growing to the length on top toward the crown, and longer hair that lies over shorter hair is cut to blend with it, so nothing hangs over the ears or the neck. Long cuts are cut to a hem, the height at which all the hair ends, with or without bangs across the forehead. Within a cut the seed sets the lengths, the parting, how far the front is swept back, lift, how tousled the locks lie, and wave. Each strand is combed away from the parting (next to it, back along it) or, in a cut worn forward, out of a whorl at the back of the crown. On top of the head it lies where the comb left it and settles onto the head after a few centimetres; where the head falls away it hangs under its weight. Long hair that leaves the head on top is pressed back onto it. It is cut where it would come down over the face; beside the face it hangs free. A strand is laid in steps of a few millimetres and stored as ten points, closer together where it bends over the head than where it hangs straight. It rests on the person's own head: how far the skin reaches from the head's middle is measured in 64 by 32 directions, ears included, so hair hangs over the ears and does not sink into the scalp; near its root a strand rises over an ear on a slope, so short hair beside the ear does not stand out. Neighbouring strands share length and bend, so hair lies in locks. The strands are stored once per person in `Scene::hair_strands` and drawn by the strand renderer as style 3: the same ribbons, read from the buffer instead of grown from the shared guide field, fading in between 4 and 40 head pixels. Grown hair casts a shadow on the face, the head and the hair under it: a quarter of each head's strands (the first, which are a fair sample) are drawn at least a texel wide into the two finest sun maps (3 m and 12 m, texels of 3 mm and 12 mm) after the shadow raster, depth only (`PlayerStrands::cast`, `strand_shadow`). Fitted people draw no groom shell; the scalp under the hair is tinted toward the hair colour (darker under dark hair, which lets little light through), lit as hair, and shows no sheen: its normal map's alpha is 0 where hair longer than a buzz stands over it, so it reflects neither sun nor sky. All of this builds up with depth behind the hairline (`Cut::under`, over the first 3 cm): at the line itself the skin is in plain view and is plain skin. The crowd keeps the cooked grooms. A fitted person's eyebrows and lashes are painted into their face texture from the seed as well: brow hairs a quarter of a millimetre wide inside the span of the brow card (which is no longer drawn for them), and a dark broken line along the rim of the upper lid. A person has an age in years: the seed draws one (18 to 78, the young the most) unless the caller gives it (`warp_human_person`'s `age`, `age = [low, high]` in a Luau game's `[humans.NAME]` pool). A face is lined by fifty: forehead lines, crow's feet, the folds (from the nose round the mouth, down from its corners, under the eyes, between the brows) and fine slack creasing on the cheeks come in between the mid twenties and fifty, and from the mid fifties they deepen to more than twice that at eighty, with a lower edge to the bag under the eye and fine lines down into the upper lip. Age also moves the face (`warp_skin::settle`), a little by fifty and most of it after: the cheeks come down and hollow, the lower cheek hangs over the jaw beside the chin, the skin under the chin sinks and loose skin hangs forward on the throat, the brow and the upper lid come down, bags stand out under the eyes, the corners of the mouth turn down, the temples hollow and the tip of the nose drops, up to 15 mm at eighty, placed by the head's own width and length. Age spots and paler lips come with old age. From the mid thirties the hair greys, first at the temples. A strand stands for many hairs, so grey mixes into every strand's colour in eight steps (stored in its shade as 2 per step) and varies in streaks a few centimetres across, never as single white strands among dark ones; the brows follow later. Everyone's hair gets sparser from the mid forties (a quarter to more than half of the strands are gone at eighty-five); in six of ten people the hairline goes back, furthest above the temples, and in half the crown thins towards bald; thin hair is mostly worn as a buzz, and the tousled and fringed cuts become rare past fifty. The body below the neck and its posture do not age, and there are no children. `games/human-lab/scripts/hair.json` captures face, crown, back and profile of all eight people.
